### 2. THE PROBLEM / CURIOSITY GAP (0:20–1:00)

**[VISUAL: Simple animated question mark or "?" motif over a calm, flat chart line]**

> Every time the market crashes, you hear the same explanation: "panic." People got scared and sold everything.
>
> But that's not really an answer — that's just describing *what* happened, not *why*.
>
> Why does panic start in the first place? Is it always triggered by the same kind of event? Or is every crash actually different underneath?
>
> To answer that, we're going to look at three of the biggest crashes in modern history — 1929, 2008, and 2020 — and find out what's actually the same, and what's actually different.

### 3. INTUITION — BUILD IT SIMPLY FIRST (1:00–2:30)

**[VISUAL: Simple illustration — a crowded room with one exit door]**

> Before the data, here's the simple version.
>
> Imagine a crowded room with one exit. As long as everyone's calm, people leave slowly, one at a time, no problem.
>
> But the moment someone shouts "fire" — real or not — everyone rushes the same exit at once. Now the exit itself becomes the danger. People get trampled not because of the fire, but because of the rush.
>
> The stock market works the same way. When investors are confident, buying and selling happens smoothly. But when confidence breaks — for any reason — everyone tries to sell at the same time. And that rush is what turns a bad day into a crash.
>
> **[TEXT DEFINITION ON SCREEN: "Bear market = when the market falls 20% or more from its recent peak"]**
>
> That's called a **bear market** — when the market falls twenty percent or more from a recent high. A crash is the sudden, fast version of that.
>
> The real question is: what makes people shout "fire" in the first place? Let's look at three different fires.

---

### 4. THE EXPLANATION — REAL EXAMPLES (2:30–5:15)

**[VISUAL: Timeline graphic — "1929" appears first]**

#### 1929 — The Slow-Motion Collapse

> Through the 1920s, the stock market had been climbing almost twenty percent a year. People got confident — so confident that many started buying stocks with borrowed money.
>
> **[TEXT DEFINITION ON SCREEN: "Margin buying = borrowing money to buy stocks, using the stocks as collateral"]**
>
> This is called **margin buying** — you put down a small amount of your own money, borrow the rest, and buy stock with it. It works great when prices go up. It's catastrophic when they go down, because a small drop can wipe out your entire investment instantly.
>
> **[VISUAL: Chart showing the Dow falling from ~305 to ~230 over 4 days]**
>
> **[TEXT DEFINITION ON SCREEN: "The Dow = an average of 30 major U.S. company stock prices, used as a quick snapshot of the market"]**
>
> Over four days in October 1929 — later called Black Thursday through Black Tuesday — the **Dow**, a snapshot index made of 30 major companies, fell twenty-five percent.
>
> But here's the part most people don't know: that four-day crash wasn't the real disaster. The real damage took three more years. By 1932, the Dow had fallen eighty-nine percent from its peak — because panic selling triggered bank runs, and banks had invested their customers' savings in the very same collapsing market.
>
> **1929's fire: too much confidence, too much borrowed money.**

**[VISUAL: Timeline graphic — "2008" appears]**

#### 2008 — The Hidden Domino

> By 2008, a major investment bank called Lehman Brothers had bet heavily on risky mortgage investments — loans given to homebuyers who, in many cases, couldn't actually afford them.
>
> When those mortgages started failing, Lehman's losses became so large that on September 15th, 2008, it filed for bankruptcy — over six hundred billion dollars in assets, the largest bankruptcy filing in U.S. history.
>
> **[TEXT DEFINITION ON SCREEN: "Bankruptcy = when a company legally admits it can't pay what it owes"]**
>
> That single event caused the Dow's worst one-day drop since 9/11. But here's the twist economists point out today: Lehman probably wasn't even the true root cause — it was just the most visible domino. Just two days later, the government had to bail out an even bigger company, AIG, proving the panic was already spreading underneath the surface before Lehman ever made headlines.
>
> **2008's fire: hidden risk nobody could see, until it was too late to stop.**

**[VISUAL: Timeline graphic — "2020" appears]**

#### 2020 — The Fastest Fire Ever

> In February 2020, the market was near an all-time high. Then COVID-19 spread globally, and economies shut down almost overnight.
>
> **[TEXT DEFINITION ON SCREEN: "S&P 500 = a broader index of 500 major U.S. companies, seen as a more accurate market snapshot than the Dow"]**
>
> The **S&P 500**, a broader and more accurate market snapshot than the Dow, fell thirty-four percent in about a month — the fastest bear market in history. On March 16th alone, the Dow dropped nearly three thousand points in a single day — the worst one-day point drop ever recorded.
>
> **[TEXT DEFINITION ON SCREEN: "Circuit breaker = an automatic trading pause triggered when the market falls too fast"]**
>
> Markets fell so fast that **circuit breakers** — automatic pauses designed to stop panic from spiraling — triggered multiple times in two weeks. Most investors alive today had never seen that happen before.
>
> But then something unusual happened: the market recovered just as fast as it fell. By August 2020, it had hit new all-time highs — the fastest recovery from a bear market ever recorded.
>
> **2020's fire: a real-world shock nobody could have prepared for.**

### 5. THE PATTERN (5:15–6:00)

**[VISUAL: Three crash charts shown side by side, then a single unifying line/text overlay]**

> Three completely different triggers. Too much confidence and debt in 1929. Hidden risk in the banking system in 2008. A global pandemic in 2020.
>
> But underneath all three, the *mechanism* was exactly the same: confidence broke, and everyone rushed for the same exit at the same time.
>
> That's the real answer. Crashes don't have one cause — they have one *pattern*. The trigger changes. The panic doesn't.
